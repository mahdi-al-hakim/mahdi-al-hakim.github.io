Three reports to Keycloak, and what separated them

Mahdi Alhakim

In early August 2026 I sent Keycloak three private security reports. All three were accepted, made public by Keycloak staff, and credited to me by name. None got a CVE. One was reclassified as an enhancement, one as a weakness handled publicly as a hardening improvement, and one as a bug, fixed and backported. They were classified differently.

This is a writeup about that: three reports to one vendor inside two weeks, what separated the one that stayed a bug from the two that did not, and what I would change about how I write a severity argument. More of it is about the reports than about the bugs.

What separated them was whose decision the behaviour already was. Impact did not separate them.

The three, with their public records.

# What Vendor's class Status
51376 OAuth2 token revocation ends a session without emitting the CAEP session-revoked event that logout emits kind/enhancement Fixed on main by PR #51386, merged 2026-10-07. Not backported
51718 A client can widen its own JWT authorization-grant audience allowlist through dynamic client registration kind/weakness Open. My proposed fix is PR #51720, open, review required
51630 A delegated admin with Clients:manage can update and disable the internal admin-permissions client kind/bug Fixed on main by PR #53883 2026-10-14 and backported to release/26.8 by PR #53958 2026-10-15

All three were originally tracked in Keycloak's private security repository and migrated into the public tracker by staff, which is why each public issue is opened by a Keycloak employee and carries an acknowledgement line rather than being filed under my own account. The fixes were written by Keycloak maintainers in every case except #51720, which is mine and is not merged.

The one that stayed a bug

ClientPermissions.isInternal decides whether a client is off limits to fine-grained admin permissions. It denylisted the realm-management client, and in the master realm any client whose id ends in -realm. It did not denylist admin-permissions, which is the resource server that holds the realm's entire fine-grained permissions model.

So a delegated administrator holding a Clients:manage permission and no realm-management roles could update and disable that client through the Admin API. Set serviceAccountsEnabled, add redirect URIs, turn on direct access grants, rename the clientId. Deleting it was correctly refused.

This one stayed a bug because the product already disagreed with itself. getAccess, the endpoint that tells an administrator what they are allowed to do, special-cased this client and returned manage: false. canManage, the enforcement path, did not have the same check and fell through to the generic permission evaluation, which granted the write. The access report said no and the enforcement said yes.

I reported it with the smallest fix I could find: add the constant for that client id to isInternal, which is how realm-management is already handled. I also wrote in the report that no privilege escalation results from it, because enforcement resolves clients by internal id rather than by clientId, and disabling the client does not relax the reporting admin's own restrictions.

Keycloak kept it as kind/bug with priority/important and fixed it in the place I named. The merged change is the maintainers' own and goes further than mine: it adds coverage on the enforcement path and on the access report, so the two cannot drift apart again. Two community pull requests proposing the same one-line change appeared on the public issue and were closed unmerged. The change that landed was written with tests.

It was backported the day after it hit main. As of 2026-10-27 it is merged to release/26.8 and labelled release/26.8.2, and 26.8.2 has not been published yet; the newest release is 26.8.1 from 2026-10-13.

The one that became an enhancement

Keycloak's Shared Signals Framework support emits a CAEP session-revoked Security Event Token when a session ends by logout. It emitted nothing when the same session ended by OAuth2 token revocation. The /revoke endpoint removes the user session server side, but the event mapper mapped only LOGOUT to that token type, and REVOKE_GRANT mapped to nothing.

The consequence depends entirely on how a relying party validates. A party that introspects every request notices immediately. A party that validates access tokens offline, which is the normal and recommended pattern, and that subscribes to CAEP specifically so it can learn about revocation in real time, is never told. It keeps honouring already-issued access tokens for that session until they expire.

I filed it as CWE-613, insufficient session expiration. Keycloak accepted the behaviour, labelled it priority/important, fixed it on main, and reclassified it as kind/enhancement.

The reclassification is correct and I can state the reasoning in one sentence, because the public issue states it: the exposure window is bounded by the access-token lifetime, and the ssf feature is experimental and off by default. A bound that is a configured token lifetime gives you a delay the deployment chose for itself. It does not give you an indefinite bypass. And a security property that only exists when you turn on a feature marked experimental is a property nobody has been promised yet.

Which is where my framing was weak. I had described this as relying parties continuing to accept revoked tokens, which is true, and I had not said clearly enough that this is also what happens with no CAEP at all. CAEP is what shortens the window. The defect is that CAEP shortened the window for one revocation path and not the other, so an operator who enabled it got an inconsistent guarantee instead of a broken one. It is a real thing to fix. It is also an enhancement to an experimental feature, and calling it a vulnerability in a shipped one was my mistake.

The fix merged to main on 2026-10-07 and was not backported. It is labelled for 27.0.0, which has not been released.

The one that became a hardening improvement, and the fix I wrote for it

The JWT authorization grant accepts an assertion from a federated identity provider and exchanges it for an access token. The audience binding is what stops an assertion issued for one relying party from being replayed at another. Keycloak reads the acceptable audiences from a client attribute, oauth2.jwt.authorization.grant.audience. When that attribute is present it replaces the server-computed default, which is the realm issuer and the token endpoint, rather than narrowing it. The same holds for the identity provider allowlist attribute.

Separately, the dynamic client registration write path copies every key from the submitted attribute map without inspecting any of them, and no client-registration policy inspects client attributes.

Put those together and a self-registered client can set its own audience allowlist to the audience of a different relying party, set its own identity provider allowlist, obtain an assertion issued for that other party, and exchange it for an access token for the federated user.

I filed it as an authorization problem. Keycloak labelled it kind/weakness and wrote the reason into the public issue: the custom audience mapping is an administrator-level configuration that is by design available at the client level, so it is handled publicly as a hardening improvement and not as a vulnerability.

I think that is right, and the reason is structural. The attribute is a configuration knob that an administrator is supposed to be able to set. There is no rule in the product saying a client may not have a custom audience list, so nothing is being bypassed when a client has one. The actual defect is narrower: dynamic client registration lets a client write a knob that was only ever meant to be set for it. The audience-replacement behaviour is the amplifier. The write path is the bug.

The distinction decides where the fix goes. If the problem is the audience semantics, you change the grant to intersect with the provider default, and you break every deployment that relies on replacement. If the problem is who may write the attribute, you gate the write. Keycloak already has the mechanism for exactly that, client policies, and the second fix breaks nothing.

So I wrote it. PR #51720 adds a client policy executor, jwt-authorization-grant-settings-disabled, that rejects requests changing either of the two attributes. It follows the existing FullScopeDisabledExecutor, handles both REGISTER and UPDATE so dynamic registration and the Admin REST API are both covered, and rejects only an actual change: an update sends the whole client representation back, so a request repeating a value the client already has still passes and unrelated edits keep working. Enabling the grant on an existing client is rejected, turning it off is allowed. Combined with the existing client-updater-context condition, the policy can be scoped to the client's own requests, leaving administrators free to configure the client normally. Six files, 664 lines added, one removed, eleven integration tests, and documentation in both the securing-apps guide and the client-policies reference next to the sibling executors.

Two limits I wrote into the pull request without waiting to be asked. Client policies only bind the paths that fire client policy events, so realm import and partial import are not covered. Both require manage-realm, which can disable the policy anyway, so the gap does not reintroduce the original problem, but it is a gap. And the executor is opt-in, like every client policy, so it hardens a deployment that enables it and does nothing for one that does not.

It has been open since 2026-08-13 and last saw activity on 2026-10-23. It is not merged, and I have no commitment from anyone that it will be.

I also disclosed in the pull request body that I used AI agents to help analyse the code paths and draft the change, and that I reviewed and verified all of it. I would rather say that up front than have a reviewer wonder.

What actually separated the three

The classes the vendor assigned do not track impact. The admin-permissions bug was the one I explicitly told the vendor had no privilege escalation in it, and it is the one that got fixed as a bug and backported inside four days of hitting main. The audience one has the most alarming attack narrative of the three and is the one still labelled a weakness.

What separated them is whether the product already contained a rule the behaviour violated.

  • #51630 violated one. getAccess said manage: false. The product had stated the intended answer and the enforcement path gave a different one. That is a bug in the ordinary sense and needs no severity argument at all. I did not have to persuade anyone of impact. I had to point at two functions that disagreed.
  • #51718 did not. Nothing in Keycloak said a client may not have a custom audience list. I was asking for a rule that did not exist yet, which is a hardening request no matter how I phrase it. Phrasing it as a bypass just meant the first thing the vendor had to do was correct my vocabulary.
  • #51376 was in between. The rule existed, in the sense that CAEP has a defined meaning, but it existed in an experimental feature that is off by default, so the product had not yet promised it to anybody.

None of that is specific to Keycloak. A report that says "these two code paths in your product disagree about the same question" is almost impossible to argue with and needs no CVSS vector. A report that says "this configuration surface should be narrower than you made it" is a design opinion, and dressing it as a bypass does not make it stronger. It makes the vendor spend their first reply on the framing instead of on the fix.

What I would do differently

Say which rule is broken, in the product's own words, before saying how bad it is. On #51630 I did that by accident, because the contradiction was so visible that it was the shortest way to describe the bug. On the other two I led with impact and let the rule be implied. That is backwards. If I cannot point at a statement the product makes and show the code not honouring it, I am proposing a new rule, and I should say so plainly and argue for it on its merits.

State the mitigating facts in the report, not after being asked. I did do this, and it is the one habit here I would keep unchanged. #51630 says in my own words that no privilege escalation results. #51718 says that assertions carrying more than one audience are refused and that assertions are single use, which limits exploitation without preventing it. #51376 notes the exposure window is bounded. Every one of those is evidence against my own severity. Including them is why the vendor's reclassifications read as agreement and not as correction. A triager who finds a limiting fact you omitted discounts everything else you wrote.

Do not argue a reclassification that is correct. Two of mine were downgraded and I accepted both without pushing back. Both downgrades were accurate, and that is the only reason I let them stand. The one thing I want from a report is that the defect gets fixed, and all three are fixed or have a fix open. Arguing a label would have cost the credibility that makes the next report get read.

Send a fix when you can tell where it goes. On #51630 the fix was one constant in one predicate and I named it; the maintainers wrote their own, with tests, in the same place. On #51718 the fix was a new executor and I wrote it. Writing it was also how I learned the framing was wrong, because building the client-policy version made it obvious that the problem was who may write the attribute and not what the attribute means. I would not have reached that by arguing about severity.

What I do not know

Whether any of these were reported by someone else first. The private reports were acknowledged and the public issues credit me by name, but Keycloak's private tracker is not visible to me and I have not tried to reconstruct it.

Whether #51720 will be merged. It is open and review is required.
